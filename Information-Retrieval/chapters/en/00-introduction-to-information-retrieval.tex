\chapter{Introduction and Architecture of Information Retrieval}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Definition and Motivations}

\textbf{Information Retrieval} (IR) is the scientific and engineering discipline concerned with representing, storing, organizing, and accessing large collections of predominantly unstructured or semi-structured data to satisfy an \textbf{information need} expressed by a user~\cite{manning2008,buttcher2010}.

\begin{definition}[Information Retrieval (Manning et al.)]
Information Retrieval is finding material of an unstructured nature (usually text) that satisfies an information need from within large collections stored on computers.
\end{definition}

IR is not limited to general-purpose Web search engines (Google, Bing): it underpins e-commerce and marketplace search, enterprise/desktop search, \textbf{RAG} (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) systems feeding Large Language Models with contextually relevant external documents, and recommender engines.

\subsection{From Web Search to Agentic Search}

The rise of conversational LLMs has not made Information Retrieval obsolete but has broadened its impact: modern LLMs continuously rely on core IR competencies (semantic query understanding, matching and ranking, contextual retrieval) to overcome their \textit{knowledge cutoff}. In the \textbf{RAG} paradigm, the system intercepts the user's request, queries an IR engine, injects the retrieved documents into the LLM's context, and generates a response supported by verifiable citations.

\begin{definition}[Agentic Search]
\textbf{Agentic Search} is a dynamic retrieval process in which an intelligent agent autonomously decides which subsequent searches to conduct, based on partial evidence gathered: it inspects sources, identifies knowledge gaps (\textit{information gaps}), formulates refined follow-up queries, and iterates until sufficient evidence has converged.
\end{definition}

\begin{alertblock}{Relevance: The Cornerstone of IR}
While in a database a record either satisfies or violates a Boolean predicate, in Information Retrieval the core challenge is estimating the degree of \textbf{relevance} with respect to the user's information need: subjective, context-dependent, and evolving over time.
\end{alertblock}

\section{The Two Macro-Objectives: Effectiveness and Efficiency}

A large-scale retrieval system pursues two objectives simultaneously, often in tension:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Effectiveness:} how accurately the ranking satisfies the user's information need.
    \item \textbf{Efficiency:} computational cost, throughput, and response latency, typically on the order of $10$--$50$ milliseconds per query over collections of billions of documents.
\end{enumerate}

The efficiency of a modern search engine depends on its interaction with the underlying hardware architecture: pipelining and superscalar processors, memory hierarchy and access locality, SIMD vectorization, and parallel/distributed computing across clusters (\textit{sharding}).

\subsection{Text Processing Pipeline}
Before indexing, every text stream goes through: \textbf{tokenization} (segmentation into lexical tokens), \textbf{normalization/case folding} (lowercasing, diacritics), \textbf{stop word filtering} (removal of high-frequency, low-discriminative words), and \textbf{stemming/lemmatization} (reducing an inflected form to its root or canonical lemma, e.g. \texttt{"searching"} $\to$ \texttt{"search"}).

\section{Language Properties and Retrieval Models}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zipf's Law:} the frequency $f(r)$ of a term is inversely proportional to its rank $r$ in the frequency table, $f(r) \propto 1/r$: a few very frequent words account for most occurrences, while most terms occur only rarely (\textit{long tail}).
    \item \textbf{Heaps' Law:} vocabulary size grows as a power law of the total number of words $N$ in the collection, $|V| = k \cdot N^\beta$ (with $0.4 \le \beta \le 0.6$): the vocabulary never fully saturates.
\end{itemize}

The main retrieval models are the \textbf{Boolean Model} (exact match via logical operators, no relevance score), the \textbf{Vector Space Model} (queries and documents as TF-IDF weighted vectors, relevance via cosine similarity), and the \textbf{Probabilistic BM25 Model}, the de facto standard for lexical retrieval:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{score}_{\text{BM25}}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \operatorname{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{tf(t, d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{tf(t, d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \dfrac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
\label{eq:bm25-formula-en}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{IDF}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{N - df_t + 0.5}{df_t + 0.5} + 1\right)$ weighs term specificity, $k_1$ (typically $1.2$--$2.0$) controls term-frequency saturation, and $b \in [0,1]$ (typically $\approx 0.75$) normalizes document length $|d|$ against the average length \text{avgdl}.

\section{The Inverted Index}

The \textbf{Inverted Index} reverses the logical orientation of document storage, realizing a mapping $t \mapsto \mathcal{P}_t$, where $\mathcal{P}_t$ is the sorted list of documents containing term $t$~\cite{manning2008}.

\begin{definition}[Inverted Index]
An Inverted Index consists of a \textbf{dictionary} (the sorted set of unique terms, with document frequency $df_t$ and a pointer to the posting list) and, for each term, a \textbf{posting list}: an array sorted by increasing DocID, whose entries may include metadata such as local term frequency ($tf$) and positional offsets.
\end{definition}

\section{Index Compression Techniques}

Inverted Index compression dramatically reduces memory footprint (both in RAM and on disk), converting an I/O bottleneck into a CPU decompression workload, which is generally much faster.

\subsection{Binary Interpolative Coding}
\textbf{Binary Interpolative Coding} (Moffat and Stuiver~\cite{moffat2000}) compresses strictly increasing sequences of integers (the DocIDs of a posting list) by recursively partitioning them: given a sublist of $n$ integers known to lie within $[l, h]$, the middle element $x_m$ (with $m = \lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor$) is encoded using $\lceil \log_2 R \rceil$ bits, where $R = h - l - n + 1$ is the number of admissible values, and the recursion proceeds separately on the two halves. When DocIDs are densely clustered, $R$ tends toward $1$ and many elements are encoded at near-zero cost.

\subsection{Dictionary-Based Compression: LZ77}
For general text, the \textbf{Lempel-Ziv '77} (LZ77) algorithm compresses via a sliding window, replacing lexical repetitions with triples composed of $(\text{offset}, \text{length}, \text{next\_char})$ referring to earlier occurrences in the window. This family of algorithms underlies widely adopted tools such as \texttt{gzip}, \texttt{7-Zip}, and \texttt{LZ4}.

\section{Learning-to-Rank (LTR) and the Non-Differentiability Challenge}

Candidates retrieved by the Inverted Index are passed to a machine learning re-ranking stage known as \textbf{Learning-to-Rank} (LTR)~\cite{burges2010}. The training dataset associates each query with a list of documents carrying graded relevance judgments $y \in \{0,\dots,4\}$ (from irrelevant to perfectly relevant); a model computes a score $s_i = f_\theta(\vec{x}_{q,d_i})$ for each query-document pair, and the final list is obtained by sorting in decreasing order of score.

\begin{alertblock}{The Cornerstone of LTR}
\textbf{The goal is to learn the ranking, not the label}: the objective is not to accurately estimate the single value $y$, but the optimal relative ordering, maximizing cumulative metrics such as \textbf{NDCG} or \textbf{MAP}.
\end{alertblock}

Because the sorting operation is discrete and step-wise, the gradient of ranking metrics with respect to model parameters is zero almost everywhere: gradient descent cannot be applied directly. The literature has developed three families of solutions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pointwise:} treats each document as an independent instance (regression or classification on $y$), ignoring the inherently comparative nature of ranking.
    \item \textbf{Pairwise} (e.g. RankNet): compares pairs of documents, modeling the probability that $d_i$ precedes $d_j$ with a differentiable sigmoid.
    \item \textbf{Listwise} (e.g. LambdaMART): optimizes the entire list via surrogate gradients $\lambda_{ij}$, weighted by the actual NDCG change resulting from swapping $d_i$ and $d_j$; combined with gradient-boosted decision trees, it was for over a decade the standard in commercial search engines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Shift Toward Neural Information Retrieval}

Since 2018, Transformer-based models (BERT) have ushered in the era of Neural Information Retrieval~\cite{khattab2020,formal2021}, pre-trained via \textbf{Masked Language Modeling}. Neural retrieval approaches fall into two families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interaction-based (Cross-Encoders):} query and document are concatenated and jointly processed by a Transformer, capturing deep interactions at high computational cost ($\BigO{|\mathcal{D}| \times \text{Transformer}}$); used only as second-stage re-rankers over a small candidate set.
    \item \textbf{Representation-based (Bi-Encoders):} query and document are independently projected into a vector space by two distinct encoders; document vectors can be pre-computed offline, reducing online matching to a dot product or an approximate nearest-neighbor search.
\end{itemize}

Among representation-based models, three families of embeddings are distinguished: \textbf{dense single-vector} (a single dense vector per document/query, queried with k-ANN graph indexes such as HNSW), \textbf{dense multi-vector} (e.g. ColBERT~\cite{khattab2020}, one vector per token with \textit{Late Interaction}/MaxSim aggregation, $S(q,d) = \sum_{t_q \in q} \max_{t_d \in d} (\vec{E}_{t_q} \cdot \vec{E}_{t_d}^T)$), and \textbf{sparse single-vector} (e.g. SPLADE~\cite{formal2021}, interpretable by design and more space/time efficient than multi-vector).

\begin{noteblock}{$k$-Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search}
For collections of millions of documents represented as high-dimensional dense vectors, exact retrieval of the $k$ most similar to the query is computationally prohibitive: index structures for \textbf{approximate nearest-neighbor search} ($k$-ANN) are used instead, trading a minimal loss of accuracy for orders-of-magnitude speedups. \textbf{TusKANNy} (\url{https://tuskanny.com/}), developed by ISTI-CNR and the University of Pisa, is a high-performance open-source example written in Rust.
\end{noteblock}

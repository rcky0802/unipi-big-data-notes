\section[Recap: Graded Relevance Evaluation Metrics]{Recap: Graded Relevance\\ Evaluation Metrics (CG, DCG, NDCG)}
\label{sec:recap-graded-relevance}

In real-world Information Retrieval systems, the binary relevance assumption (a document is either relevant or non-relevant) fails to capture qualitative nuances across retrieved results. Relevance judgments are instead recorded on discrete graded scales (e.g., Likert scales from $0$ to $3$ or $0$ to $4$, reflecting increasing degrees of pertinence).

Evaluating a ranked list at cutoff depth $p$ relies on two core principles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gain:} placing highly relevant documents at top positions of the Search Engine Results Page (SERP) significantly boosts user satisfaction.
    \item \textbf{Positional Discounting:} relevant documents placed at lower ranks yield diminishing perceived utility, since human attention decays monotonically down the list.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cumulative Gain (CG) and Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)}

Given a graded relevance vector $\mathbf{r} = [r_1, r_2, \dots, r_p]$ for the top-$p$ documents retrieved for a query $q$:

\begin{definition}[Cumulative Gain at Rank $p$]
\textbf{Cumulative Gain} ($CG_p$) sums relevance scores linearly up to rank $p$:
\begin{equation}
CG_p = \sum_{i=1}^{p} r_i.
\end{equation}
\textit{Structural limitation:} $CG_p$ is position-insensitive: swapping an essential document ($r=3$) between position 1 and position 10 leaves $CG$ unchanged.
\end{definition}

To model user attention decay across rank positions $i$, logarithmic discount functions are applied:

\begin{definition}[Discounted Cumulative Gain at Rank $p$]
Under the standard formulation and the exponential gain variant widespread in commercial web search:
\begin{align}
\text{DCG}_p &= r_1 + \sum_{i=2}^{p} \frac{r_i}{\log_2(i)}, \label{eq:dcg-std} \\
\text{DCG}_p^{\text{exp}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{2^{r_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}. \label{eq:dcg-exp}
\end{align}
The exponential formulation~\eqref{eq:dcg-exp} heavily rewards placing top-relevance documents at the very highest ranks.
\end{definition}

\subsection[Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)]{Normalized Discounted Cumulative\\ Gain (NDCG) and Query Normalization}

Raw DCG values are strictly \textbf{query-dependent}. If query $q_A$ has 100 relevant documents in the corpus, its theoretical maximum DCG will be huge; if query $q_B$ has only one relevant document, its maximum DCG will be small. Averaging raw DCG across different queries is therefore mathematically invalid.

To enable benchmark comparability (\textit{macro-averaging}), \textbf{Ideal DCG} ($\text{IDCG}_p$) is defined as the DCG of the ideal ranking obtained by sorting all documents by descending relevance up to depth $p$:
\begin{equation}
\text{NDCG}_p = \frac{\text{DCG}_p}{\text{IDCG}_p} \quad \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}
An $\text{NDCG}_p$ of $1.0$ guarantees that the algorithm achieved the best possible ordering up to depth $p$.

\section{Fundamental Properties of Natural Language}
\label{sec:language-properties}

\subsection{Distinctive Characteristics of Human Language}
Automated processing of natural language faces structural bottlenecks inherent to human linguistic systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thousands of discrete symbols:} lexicons consist of hundreds of thousands of distinct words and morphological stems, expanding with domain-specific terms and neologisms.
    \item \textbf{Complex and recursive syntax:} sentences form hierarchical dependency trees with long-distance syntactic bindings.
    \item \textbf{Predominantly compositional semantics:} overall sentence meaning derives from combining constituent units. Notable exceptions include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Collocations:} conventional recurrent phrases (e.g., \textit{``make the bed''}, \textit{``skip class''}).
        \item \textit{Idiomatic expressions:} figurative meanings not deductible from individual constituents (e.g., \textit{``break a leg''}, \textit{``out of the frying pan into the fire''}).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pervasive ambiguity:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Part-of-Speech (POS) Ambiguity:} identical words serving distinct grammatical roles depending on context (e.g., \textit{``beat''}: noun in \textit{``Listen to this cool beat''}, verb in \textit{``I'll beat you at checkers''}).
        \item \textit{Word Sense Ambiguity (Polysemy and Homonymy):} multiple meanings for a single surface form (e.g., \textit{``interest''}: intellectual curiosity vs financial interest rate).
        \item \textit{Syntactic Ambiguity:} multiple valid parse trees. Groucho Marx's famous joke illustrates this: \textit{``I shot an elephant in my pajamas''} (the prepositional phrase can attach to the subject or the object).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shared world knowledge dependence:} comprehension relies on extra-textual knowledge. \textit{``I'm faster than Lewis Hamilton''} requires knowing Hamilton is an F1 driver; \textit{``I was on a bike with a helmet''} implies wearing the helmet, whereas \textit{``I was on a bike with an electric motor''} implies the motor is mounted on the bike.
    \item \textbf{Continuous temporal evolution:} semantic drift (e.g., \textit{``in the cloud''}) and rapid emergence of technological and cultural neologisms (\textit{``to google''}, \textit{``crowdfunded''}, \textit{``super spreader''}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Zipf's Law]
Sorting corpus terms by descending frequency, the term at rank $r$ has empirical frequency $f$ inversely proportional to $r$:
\begin{equation}
f \propto \frac{1}{r} \implies f \cdot r \approx C,
\end{equation}
where $C$ is a corpus-specific scaling constant.
\end{definition}

In Mandelbrot's generalized formulation (\textit{Zipf-Mandelbrot law}):
\begin{equation}
f(r) \propto \frac{1}{(r + b)^a},
\end{equation}
with empirical parameters typically fitted around $a \approx 1$ and $b \approx 2.7$.

\begin{noteblock}{Principle of Least Effort}
Zipf theorized this distribution emerges from an economic equilibrium between competing cognitive drivers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Speaker's Effort:} minimizes articulatory expense using a compact vocabulary of short, frequent, polysemous words.
    \item \textbf{Hearer's Effort:} favors an expansive vocabulary of rare, distinct, non-ambiguous words to minimize semantic disambiguation cost.
\end{enumerate}
The evolutionary balance produces the observed hyperbolic curve.
\end{noteblock}

Two verifiable empirical consequences follow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the standard benchmark corpus \textit{Reuters 21578} ($\approx 500\,000$ total word occurrences), just \textbf{313 distinct words} account for over $50\%$ of all token occurrences.
    \item A word's number of distinct dictionary meanings $m$ is inversely proportional to the square root of its frequency: $m \propto 1 / \sqrt{f}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Rare Terms and Stop Words}
Zipf's distribution exposes a clear vocabulary bifurcation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stop Words:} ultra-high frequency words at minimum ranks ($r \le 100$), mostly function words, articles, prepositions, and conjunctions (\textit{the}, \textit{of}, \textit{a}, \textit{in}, \textit{to}). They carry negligible topical information for discriminating documents. However, aggressive stripping harms phrase search (e.g., \textit{``to be or not to be''}) and sentiment classification.
    \item \textbf{Rare Terms (Hapax Legomena):} words occurring only once or twice across the collection. They constitute $40\%$--$50\%$ of unique vocabulary terms, encompassing spelling errors (\textit{typos}), technical slugs, or rare keywords with very high discriminatory power.
\end{enumerate}

\section[Text Acquisition and Preprocessing Pipeline]{Text Acquisition and\\ Preprocessing Pipeline}
\label{sec:preprocessing-pipeline-en}

\subsection{Web Text Extraction}
Search engine pipelines start by ingesting raw web data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Document Fetching (Network I/O):} Python's \texttt{urllib.request} issues HTTP requests to pull raw HTML payloads:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythoncode,caption={Downloading web markup using urllib.}]
from urllib import request

url = "http://example.org/page.html"
response = request.urlopen(url)
html = response.read().decode('utf8')
\end{lstlisting}

    \item \textbf{HTML Stripping and Text Extraction:} HTML formatting markup, layout attributes, and scripts represent non-informative noise. Dedicated parsers like \texttt{BeautifulSoup} isolate textual content:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythoncode,caption={Isolating clean textual content using BeautifulSoup.}]
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup

soup = BeautifulSoup(html, "html5lib")
# Global unstructured text extraction
raw_text = soup.get_text()

# Structured paragraph-level extraction
paragraphs = [' '.join(s.findAll(text=True)) for s in soup.findAll('p')]
\end{lstlisting}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Segmentation and Tokenization}
\label{sec:tokeniz-splitting-en}

\paragraph{Sentence Splitting.}
Sentence boundaries cannot be determined simply by period punctuation (\texttt{.}). Periods appear in acronyms (\textit{U.S.A.}), honorific abbreviations (\textit{Dr.}, \textit{St.}), decimal numbers (\textit{8.30}), and ellipses. NLTK's \texttt{sent\_tokenize()} employs a trained classifier to distinguish contextual punctuation roles.

\paragraph{Word Tokenization.}
Splits the character stream into discrete lexical units (\textit{tokens}):
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythoncode,caption={Standard word tokenization with NLTK.}]
from nltk import word_tokenize

tokens = word_tokenize("The President of the United States of America")
# Output: ['The', 'President', 'of', 'the', 'United', 'States', 'of', 'America']
\end{lstlisting}
Specialized tokenizers handle specific domains: \texttt{TweetTokenizer} for hashtags, handles, and emojis, or \texttt{RegexpTokenizer} for regular-expression-driven extraction.

\subsection[Morphological Normalization: Stemming vs. Lemmatization]{Morphological Normalization: Stemming vs.\\ Lemmatization}
\label{sec:stemming-lemmatizzazione-en}

Morphological normalization collapses inflected and derived surface variants into a canonical representation, shrinking index vocabulary dimensionality.

\subsubsection{Stemming}
Heuristic rule-based string transformation (stripping or substituting affixes):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Properties:} highly computationally efficient, operates on individual words without requiring dictionaries or syntax trees.
    \item \textbf{Pitfalls:} frequently produces non-existent root stems (\textit{overstemming} and \textit{understemming}).
    \item \textbf{Example (Porter Stemmer):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{"cars"} $\to$ \texttt{"car"} (correct plural stripping).
        \item \texttt{"was"} $\to$ \texttt{"wa"} (the algorithm mistakenly strips the trailing \texttt{s} as an inflectional plural suffix).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Lemmatization}
Principled morphological process that maps inflected surface tokens to their authentic dictionary lemma:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Properties:} leverages \textit{Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging} and comprehensive lexical resources (such as \textit{WordNet}).
    \item \textbf{Example (\texttt{WordNetLemmatizer}):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Default call without POS (treated as noun): \texttt{lemmatize("was")} outputs \texttt{"wa"}.
        \item Specifying proper POS (verb): \texttt{lemmatize("was", pos='v')} returns the dictionary lemma \texttt{"be"}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Representation Models: Bag of Words and N-Grams}
\label{sec:bow-ngrams-en}

\subsection{The Bag of Words (BoW) Model}
The Bag of Words model discards syntactic ordering and grammatical dependencies, abstracting each document into an unordered multiset (or set) of independent tokens:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythoncode,caption={Basic set-based text representation.}]
from nltk import word_tokenize

text = 'the president of the united states of america'
tokens = word_tokenize(text)  # Length: 8 tokens
bow_set = set(tokens)         # Cardinality: 6 unique elements
# bow_set: {'america', 'of', 'president', 'states', 'the', 'united'}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Structural Limitations of Bag of Words}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loss of syntactic order and compositional semantics:}
    Consider two statements with completely opposite real-world meanings:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $t_1$: \textit{``I won, and thus you lose.''}
        \item $t_2$: \textit{``I lose, and thus you won.''}
    \end{itemize}
    Under set-based BoW representation:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{set}(t_1) \equiv \text{set}(t_2) = \{\text{'and'}, \text{'lose'}, \text{'thus'}, \text{'won'}, \text{'you'}\}.
    \end{equation}
    To the retrieval system, these opposite assertions are completely identical, causing \textbf{semantic collapse}.
    \item \textbf{Loss of multiplicity (in binary set versions):} fails to record how many times a key term appears.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{N-Gram Models}
To recover local context without bearing full syntactic parsing overhead, vocabularies are enriched with \textbf{$n$-grams} (contiguous sequences of $n$ items).

\subsubsection{Word N-grams}
Extracts sequences of $n$ consecutive words ($n=2$ for bigrams, $n=3$ for trigrams), retaining local word order:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Applied to the victory/loss example ($n=2$):
    \begin{align}
    \text{ngrams}(t_1, 2) &= \{\text{'I won'}, \text{'won and'}, \text{'and thus'}, \text{'thus you'}, \text{'you lose'}\}, \\
    \text{ngrams}(t_2, 2) &= \{\text{'I lose'}, \text{'lose and'}, \text{'and thus'}, \text{'thus you'}, \text{'you won'}\}.
    \end{align}
    Now the representations differ ($\text{ngrams}(t_1, 2) \ne \text{ngrams}(t_2, 2)$), preserving who won and who lost.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Character N-grams}
Extracts substrings of $n$ consecutive characters within words (commonly $n=3$ trigrams):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Role in IR:} provides resilience against typing errors (\textit{typo-tolerance}) and captures morphological similarities.
    \item \textbf{Example with typographical error} (\texttt{"rainbow"} vs. \texttt{"rainbaw"}):
    \begin{align}
    C_3(\text{"rainbow"}) &= \{\text{'r-a-i'}, \text{'a-i-n'}, \text{'i-n-b'}, \text{'n-b-o'}, \text{'b-o-w'}\}, \\
    C_3(\text{"rainbaw"}) &= \{\text{'r-a-i'}, \text{'a-i-n'}, \text{'i-n-b'}, \text{'n-b-a'}, \text{'b-a-w'}\}.
    \end{align}
    The intersection is non-empty:
    \begin{equation}
    C_3(\text{"rainbow"}) \cap C_3(\text{"rainbaw"}) = \{\text{'r-a-i'}, \text{'a-i-n'}, \text{'i-n-b'}\}.
    \end{equation}
    Sharing 3 out of 5 trigrams ($60\%$), the search engine identifies high proximity and suggests \textit{``Did you mean: rainbow?''}.
\end{itemize}
